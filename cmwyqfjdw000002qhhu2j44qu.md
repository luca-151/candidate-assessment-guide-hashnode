# Skills-Based Hiring in 2026: What Employers Say, What They Do, and What Candidates Should Know

*Last checked: 27 November 2026 · 9 min read*

> **How we cover this.** This is a category spotlight, not a comparison, so there is no scored ranking here. Every number below comes from a named source, and the text says which one. Where a figure comes from a vendor survey, the text says that too, because a testing company reporting that testing is popular deserves a sceptical read.

## The headline numbers do not agree with each other

Ask employers whether they hire on skills, and the answer is an overwhelming yes. TestGorilla's State of Skills-Based Hiring 2025 report, based on a survey of 1,084 employers and 1,076 job seekers in the US and UK in April 2025, found that 85% of employers say they use skills-based hiring, up from 81% in 2024 and 73% in 2023. More than half, 53%, said they had removed degree requirements from at least some roles, up from 30% a year earlier. The National Association of Colleges and Employers (NACE) found a similar picture for entry-level hiring specifically: its Job Outlook 2026 survey found 70% of employers use skills-based hiring for entry-level roles, up from 65% the year before.

Now look at what actually happened to hiring. Harvard Business School and the Burning Glass Institute studied 11,300 roles at large US firms between 2014 and 2023, tracking what changed after employers removed degree requirements. Their report, "Skills-Based Hiring: The Long Road from Pronouncements to Practice" (February 2024), found that the number of roles dropping degree requirements nearly quadrupled over the period, yet hiring of workers without a bachelor's degree rose by only 3.5 percentage points in those roles. Because the change applied to only 3.6% of roles, the net effect was 0.14 percentage points across the market. In 2023, about 97,000 workers out of 77 million yearly hires benefited. Fewer than 1 in 700 hires.

Both sets of numbers can be true at once. That is the story of skills-based hiring in 2026: a real shift, adopted unevenly, and often announced far more boldly than it is practised.

## What skills-based hiring actually means

Skills-based hiring is not one policy. It is a spectrum of practices, and the label covers all of them, which is part of why the numbers look so strong.

At the lightest end, an employer removes the degree requirement from a job posting and changes nothing else. At the middle, the employer adds a skills test or a structured interview to a process that still starts with a resume screen. At the serious end, the employer rebuilds the process: the resume stops being the first filter, candidates are assessed on work samples or job simulations before anyone looks at their education, and hiring managers are retrained to evaluate what a candidate can do rather than where they studied.

The TestGorilla survey shows where most employers sit on that spectrum. 76% use skills tests at some stage, but only 36% test candidates before screening resumes, and 67% still use resumes. In other words, for most employers, skills testing is an extra step inside a traditional process, not a replacement for it. The iCIMS Workforce Report of June 2025 found the same tension from the recruiter side: 95% of recruiters surveyed said they used skills-based practices, but only 28% ranked skills as the leading factor for entry-level candidates, behind experience at 37% and education at 34%.

None of this makes skills-based hiring a fiction. It makes it a partial reform, and candidates who understand which end of the spectrum an employer sits on have a large advantage over candidates who take the headline at face value.

## The gap between policy and practice

The Harvard and Burning Glass researchers sorted the companies they studied into three groups, and the split is where the argument lands.

**37% were genuine adopters.** Nearly all of the actual change in hiring behaviour happened at just over a third of the firms studied. These companies retrained hiring managers, rebuilt screening, and hired measurably more workers without degrees. The report names Apple, Cigna, ExxonMobil, General Motors, Koch Industries, Target, Tyson Foods, Walmart, Yelp, the State of Minnesota and the City of Denver among the firms whose behaviour changed.

**45% changed in name only.** They removed degree requirements from postings and continued selecting degree holders at the same rate. The posting changed; the hiring did not.

**18% were backsliders.** They tried skills-based hiring and then reverted to requiring degrees.

The study's lead author, Harvard Business School professor Joseph Fuller, put it plainly: "We found measurable but pretty modest changes, and that some companies have done better than others."

There is an important caveat about the evidence itself. TestGorilla sells skills tests, so its survey has an interest in the trend it measures. That does not make its numbers wrong, and its year-on-year trend (73% to 81% to 85%) is consistent with other surveys, including NACE's. But the strongest evidence for the gap comes from the independent side: Harvard and Burning Glass measured hiring outcomes, not survey answers, and the outcomes barely moved.

## The employers actually doing it

A few large employers have built skills-based hiring into real pipelines rather than press releases, and they are worth knowing by name because they are where the opportunities actually are.

**Accenture** has made apprenticeships a core part of its entry-level strategy. The firm targets sourcing 20% of its US and Canada entry-level hires through apprenticeship programmes, and has hit that mark for three consecutive fiscal years, according to 2026 reporting on the programme. Apprentices train full-time in fields including cybersecurity, data engineering, cloud engineering and IT support, and the programme is open to candidates without a four-year degree.

**IBM** runs one of the most established apprenticeship programmes in enterprise tech: a full-time, paid programme that does not require a bachelor's degree. The company does not require a bachelor's degree for roughly half of its US roles, and about 15% of IBM's US hires did not hold a four-year degree, according to IBM's vice president of talent Joanna Daley, speaking to CNBC.

**EY** removed degree requirements for entry-level positions in 2016, a decade before the current wave, and evaluates candidates through skills assessments and structured interviews instead.

Governments have moved too. Pennsylvania opened 92% of state government positions to candidates without degrees, and Maryland reported a 41% increase in hires in the first year after removing degree filters. The World Economic Forum's Future of Jobs Report 2025 found that 81% of employers now expect to prioritise demonstrated work experience over university degrees when evaluating candidates.

These are genuine pipelines, but they are concentrated. Skills-based hiring is most established in tech, consulting, engineering and the public sector, and much thinner elsewhere. A candidate's chances depend heavily on which sector and which employer they target.

## What it changes for candidates

If you are applying for entry-level roles in 2026, here is what the evidence supports.

**Lead with proof of work, not with your degree or its absence.** 71% of employers in the TestGorilla survey said skills tests predict on-the-job success better than resumes. Work samples, portfolios, coding assessments and structured test scores are the currency that the genuine adopters actually accept. A short portfolio of finished work does more in these processes than a longer list of qualifications.

**Apply to the employers that changed behaviour, not just the ones that changed postings.** The 37% genuine adopters are identifiable: they run apprenticeships, publish skills-based hiring targets, or assess before they screen resumes. Accenture's apprenticeship route and IBM's programme are public and open. A posting that no longer mentions a degree but still asks for a resume first and tests last is, on the evidence, probably a name-only change.

**Expect a test, and prepare for it like the real filter it is.** With 76% of employers using skills tests, the assessment is often the stage that decides the outcome, and it is the stage candidates prepare for least. The tests themselves vary: role-specific tests and cognitive ability tests are the most common formats, according to TestGorilla's 2025 survey. Practising the format matters more than studying the subject, because timed, unfamiliar test formats punish first-time takers.

**Consider apprenticeships as a parallel route, not a fallback.** Apprenticeships are the most concrete alternative the skills-based shift has produced. The US has about 940,000 active apprentices, and the large employer programmes pay competitive first-year wages while training on the job. For candidates without a degree, or with a degree in an unrelated subject, an apprenticeship at a genuine adopter is often a faster route into a career than another round of applications to firms that still hire on credentials.

**Do not assume the degree no longer matters.** The same surveys that show adoption also show its limits. 41% of employers in the TestGorilla survey said degrees are more important than five years ago, and the Harvard and Burning Glass data shows degree holders still win the overwhelming majority of roles at firms that dropped requirements. The degree is a weaker filter than it was. It is not a dead one.

## What to watch next

Three things will decide whether skills-based hiring becomes the norm or stalls as a half-kept promise. First, whether more employers move testing before resume screening: only 36% do today, and until that number rises, the resume remains the real first filter. Second, whether regulators and large public-sector employers keep pushing, since government hiring is where the largest measurable changes have happened so far. Third, whether candidates can tell the difference: the more applicants learn to spot genuine adopters, the more pressure name-only employers face to follow through.
