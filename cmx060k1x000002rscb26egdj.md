# Josh Bersin: The Analyst Who Shapes What Employers Do Next

*Last checked: 28 November 2026 · 9 min read*

> **How we cover this.** This is a Leaders in Hiring profile. Every fact comes from a named public source, listed at the end, and the profile says where a claim is an interpretation rather than a documented fact. No quotes are invented and no views are attributed to Josh Bersin that he has not published himself.

## The analyst behind the employer's playbook

If you have ever wondered why so many employers suddenly talk about skills instead of degrees, or why a job advert now asks for adaptability above all else, or why your interview is run by an AI screening tool, there is a good chance the idea reached your employer through Josh Bersin.

Bersin is an American industry analyst, author and educator who has spent more than two decades researching how companies hire, train and manage people. He is not a recruiter and he does not run a job board. His audience is employers and HR leaders, and his influence works one step removed from candidates: his research shapes what companies buy, what their HR teams are told to prioritise, and, in the end, what the hiring process you walk into actually looks like.

That is why he belongs in a series about the people who shape hiring, and why candidates should know his name even though his work is not written for them.

## An engineer who ended up explaining HR

Bersin was born in 1956 in Brookline, Massachusetts. His background is engineering, not human resources. He took a Bachelor of Science in engineering from Cornell University in 1978, a Master of Science in engineering from Stanford University in 1982, and an MBA from the Haas School of Business at the University of California, Berkeley, in 1988.

Before he founded his own firm, he spent about 25 years in product development, product management, marketing and sales at IBM and Sybase and at Silicon Valley e-learning startups. That matters for understanding his work. He came to HR from the technology side, and his research has always treated HR as a problem of systems, data and design rather than as a purely human one. When he writes about hiring, he writes as someone who spent a career building and selling the software that employers use.

## Bersin and Associates, and the Deloitte years

In 2001 Bersin founded Bersin and Associates, a research and advisory firm focused at first on corporate learning and later expanding into HR, talent management, talent acquisition and leadership. The model was straightforward: survey large employers, study what the best performers do differently, and sell the findings back to the industry as research, benchmarks and advice.

It worked. The firm grew into one of the best known names in HR research, and in 2012 Bersin sold it to Deloitte. It became Bersin by Deloitte, and Bersin stayed on as a Deloitte principal until 2018. During those years he was the principal author and architect of the Deloitte Human Capital Trends study, the firm's flagship annual survey of HR and business leaders around the world. That study, published every year and read by thousands of executives, is one of the main channels through which his framing of hiring and work reaches boardrooms.

The Deloitte years made Bersin the closest thing HR has to a household name among executives. They also illustrate a pattern that runs through his career: he builds an audience by naming a shift early, giving it a memorable label, and then documenting it with survey data.

## The ideas that travelled

Two of his labels are worth knowing because they still shape the language employers use. The first is "learning in the flow of work", the idea that training works best when it happens inside the job, in small pieces, at the moment it is needed, rather than in a classroom removed from it. The second is "The Big Reset", his framing of the post-pandemic rethink of work, in which employers were forced to redesign jobs, pay and flexibility at once.

His current framing is the "Superworker": workers amplified by AI rather than replaced by it. It is the central idea of his latest work, and it is the clearest sign of where his thinking has moved. Where much of the public debate about AI and hiring asks which jobs will disappear, Bersin's argument is that AI changes what a worker can do, and that employers should redesign roles around that rather than simply cut headcount. Candidates will meet this idea in practice: employers that adopt it look for people who can work with AI tools, not only people who can do the task the tool now does faster.

## The books

Bersin has written four business books. The first two, The Blended Learning Book and The Training Measurement Book, came out of his early work on corporate learning. The third, Irresistible: The Seven Secrets of the World's Most Enduring, Employee-Focused Organizations, was published in 2022 and won a Best Indie Book Award. Its argument is that the most durable companies are the ones built around their employees, and it distils his research into seven principles for employers.

The fourth, Superpowered: How AI Unlocks the Superpower of People, Leadership, and Teams, was published in October 2026 and written with Kathi Enderes. It carries the Superworker idea into book form, arguing that AI should be treated as a capability that extends what people can do. For candidates, the book is less useful as direct advice than as a preview: the employers reading it are the ones deciding how AI will be used in their hiring and in the jobs they offer.

## The independent second act

In 2019 Bersin left the large-firm world behind and started again as an independent. He founded the Josh Bersin Academy that year, a global development academy for HR and talent professionals, and then The Josh Bersin Company in 2019/2020, where he is chief executive.

The move matters because it mirrors the change in HR itself. The first firm sold research reports to large employers. The new company sells something different: Galileo, an AI agent built for HR professionals, and Galileo Learn, a co-learning community with more than 50,000 members. Instead of publishing a report once a year and waiting for executives to read it, the company now offers a tool that HR teams can question directly, and a community where practitioners learn from each other.

It is also a measure of his reach. He has nearly one million followers on LinkedIn, an audience few HR analysts can match, and his daily commentary is read by the people who run hiring at large employers. When he writes that a practice is outdated, it is not a private opinion. It is a signal to an industry that takes its cues from him.

## How one analyst moves a whole industry

It is worth pausing on the mechanics, because they explain why a single analyst can matter so much to candidates who will never read his work. The influence runs through three channels, and Bersin has used all three across his career.

The first is the annual study. The Deloitte Human Capital Trends study, which Bersin shaped as principal author and architect, surveys thousands of business and HR leaders each year and reports what they say they are doing and planning. When a study like that reports that most employers are moving to skills-based hiring or investing in AI, it does more than describe a trend. It gives every HR leader who reads it permission to do the same, and a number to quote to their chief executive when they ask for budget. The study becomes the evidence that justifies the change, and the change then shows up in the job adverts and assessments candidates see.

The second channel is the conference stage. Bersin speaks at the large HR conferences where practitioners gather, and his talks are often the ones that set the vocabulary for the year. A phrase coined on a stage in spring can be in a job description by autumn. Candidates who notice new words appearing in adverts, such as skills, agility or, now, Superworker, are often seeing the vocabulary of the conference circuit filtering down.

The third channel is the vendor market. HR technology vendors read the same research candidates never see, and they build their products around it. When analysts say the future is skills-based hiring, vendors add skills features to their software, and employers who buy that software inherit the analyst's assumptions in the tools they use to screen applications. The influence is indirect but it is real: the questions you are asked in an assessment were often written by a vendor that was following an analyst's lead.

Understanding this chain does not change the process you face, but it changes how you read it. A hiring trend is rarely a law of nature. It is usually a research finding, repeated at conferences, built into software, and then presented to candidates as the way things are done.

## Why his work reaches your interview

None of this is candidate advice, and that distinction is the most important thing to carry away from this profile. Bersin's work is written for employers and HR leaders. It tells them what to buy, what to measure and what to change. Candidates are the subject of that research, not its audience.

That does not make it irrelevant to candidates. It makes it a window. When a large employer's hiring team changes its process, it usually does so because a report, a conference talk or an analyst's framework told them to. The spread of skills-based hiring, the growth of AI screening, the emphasis on internal mobility and the current enthusiasm for redesigning jobs around AI all passed through the kind of research Bersin produces. If you understand what employers are being told, you understand the logic of the process you are being assessed in, even when that process feels arbitrary from the candidate side.

There is a fair criticism to make here, and it should be stated plainly. Analyst research describes what leading employers do, and leading employers are usually large, well resourced companies. The practices that look sensible for a global firm with a dedicated HR team do not always fit a small employer, and they do not always serve candidates well. AI screening, for example, is often adopted because analysts and vendors present it as efficient, while candidates experience it as opaque. Bersin's own framing, the Superworker, is optimistic about AI in a way that many candidates, whose applications are filtered by machines before a human ever sees them, may not share. His work is a good guide to what employers are being told. It is not a neutral account of what candidates experience.

## What it means for candidates

**Read employer research as a forecast, not as advice.** When Bersin and other analysts describe a shift, such as skills-based hiring or AI-assisted work, employers act on it within a year or two. Knowing the direction of travel lets you prepare for the process that is coming, not only the one in front of you.

**Learn to work with AI tools, and be ready to show it.** The Superworker idea is now the framing many large employers use for AI. If you can demonstrate that you use AI to do better work, rather than to avoid work, you are speaking the language hiring teams are being taught.

**Expect the assessment to keep changing.** The firms that follow analyst research redesign their hiring regularly. A process that was standard two years ago may already be out of date in the eyes of the people who run it. Prepare for assessments, video interviews and skills tests as a moving target, not a fixed exam.

**Remember who the research is for.** When a hiring process feels designed for the employer's convenience rather than yours, it often is. Analyst research optimises for the buyer. Understanding that helps you read the process clearly: you are being measured against a model of the ideal employee that was built for employers, and your job is to show how you fit it, not to assume it was built for you.
