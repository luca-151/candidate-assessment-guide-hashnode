# Multiverse: The Apprenticeship Unicorn at the Crossroads of Degrees and Skills

*Last checked: 28 November 2026 · 9 min read*

> **How we cover this.** This is an institution profile. Every figure comes from a named source, either the company itself, a regulator or official statistics, or reporting that attributes the figure to one of those. Where the company's own numbers and the official numbers measure different things, the text says so rather than picking the friendlier figure. No one quoted here is quoted from anything other than published material.

## A company built on a bet against the degree

Multiverse is a British education technology company based in London. It was founded in 2016 by Euan Blair and Sophie Adelman, and it launched under the name WhiteHat. In January 2021 it rebranded to Multiverse, a name that matched a bigger ambition than the one it started with.

The original idea was simple and, at the time, contrarian. Take school leavers who had not gone to university and match them with technology apprenticeships at real employers, as an alternative to a degree. The pitch to young people was that they could earn, learn and build a career without student debt. The pitch to employers was that they could hire for potential rather than credentials, and reach talent their graduate schemes never saw.

Early employers included Facebook, BP, Santander and Publicis. By 2019, more than 250 employers had launched programmes with the company. The model addressed a real gap: Britain's apprenticeship system was built for trades, and the technology industry had barely used it.

That is the company Multiverse still describes itself as in broad terms. What it actually does, and who it does it for, has changed a great deal since 2016. That change is the story of this profile, and it sits exactly on the fault line this publication covers: the shift from degrees to skills, and from entry-level hiring to the AI upskilling of people already in work.

## The money first: more than $400 million raised

Multiverse is the best funded education technology company in British history, and the funding timeline is the clearest way to see how investor expectations have shaped it.

![Funding and valuation timeline: founded as WhiteHat in 2016, $16M Series A in 2019, rebrand to Multiverse and $44M in 2021, $130M Series C at an $875M valuation in 2021, $220M Series D at a $1.7B valuation in 2022, $70M at a $2.1B valuation in 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/luca-151/candidate-assessment-guide-hashnode/main/images/article-07-funding.svg align="center")

The Series A came in 2019: $16 million led by Index Ventures. In January 2021, the same month as the rebrand, General Catalyst led a $44 million round. Later that year a $130 million Series C valued the company at $875 million. Then, in June 2022, a $220 million Series D at a $1.7 billion valuation made Multiverse the UK's first edtech unicorn. In May 2026, Schroders Capital led a $70 million round at a $2.1 billion valuation.

In total, the company has raised more than $400 million. That scale of funding matters for how to read everything that follows, because it sets the terms of the story. A company that has taken $400 million is not judged, by its investors or by the market, on whether it runs good apprenticeships. It is judged on whether it becomes a very large business. The pivot described below, from placing school leavers into first jobs toward upskilling existing employees, is best understood in that light: the second market is far bigger than the first.

The financials published so far show a company still spending heavily to get there. Revenue was £79.6 million in the year to March 2025, up from £58.4 million the year before, a rise of about 36%. In the same accounts, the pre-tax loss widened to £63.3 million. Headcount stood at about 813 staff in January 2026. The company reported its first cash-positive quarter in 2026, which is a meaningful milestone for a business that has burned through hundreds of millions, though one quarter is not yet a pattern.

## What it actually sells now

Today Multiverse sells apprenticeship and upskilling programmes to employers, mainly in AI, data and software engineering. Programmes run at levels 3 to 6, which in the English system runs from the equivalent of A levels up to degree level, and last from 11 to 38 months. Learners get coaches, live sessions and an AI coaching platform called Atlas.

The funding model is the part candidates most need to understand. Employers in England pay from their apprenticeship levy, the tax on large employers that funds apprenticeship training. Learners pay nothing. That is a genuine advantage over a degree, and it is the core of the original pitch: a route into skilled work with no tuition debt.

But the structure of the market has consequences. Because the employer pays, the employer chooses. The learner is the beneficiary of the programme, not the customer. That shapes who gets in, what is taught and what counts as success, and it is why the completion and quality figures in the second half of this article matter as much as the headline scale.

The company also now awards its own degrees. It awarded its first own BSc degrees in November 2024, which is a striking turn for a company founded to offer an alternative to degrees. The logic is consistent enough: if employers and learners both value a degree-level credential, owning the credential is valuable. But it also shows how far the original positioning has blurred. Multiverse now sits across the line it once drew, offering degree-level study through an apprenticeship route.

## The pivot: from school leavers to people already in work

From 2023, the focus moved. The company that began by placing school leavers into first jobs increasingly sold upskilling to employers for their existing staff, and increasingly around AI. The shift was not presented as a retreat from the original mission, and it is fair to say the two markets overlap. But the emphasis changed, and the change showed up in the staffing: in November 2023 it cut up to 44 jobs, mostly in the US, as it reshaped the business.

The acquisitions tell the same story. Eduflow, a learning platform, was acquired in 2023. Searchlight, a US AI hiring startup, followed in 2024. In January 2026 Multiverse acquired StackFuel, a Berlin data and AI training provider, with a stated goal of training 100,000 German workers in AI skills and a reported 92% programme completion rate for StackFuel's programmes. The pattern is consistent: buy capability in AI training, and buy it where the demand is, which is increasingly among people already employed.

There is a real commercial logic to this. The school leaver market is small, seasonal and expensive to serve: each placement needs an employer willing to take a chance on an 18-year-old, and a young person willing to forgo university. The upskilling market is the opposite. Employers already have the staff, already have levy funds to spend, and already feel pressure to make their workforce AI literate. A company that can sell to an employer's whole workforce rather than to a handful of school leavers each year has a much larger addressable market, and that is what a $2.1 billion valuation demands.

The cost is that the original mission becomes a smaller part of the business. School leavers face a different market than the one WhiteHat launched into in 2016. Apprenticeships increasingly go to existing employees, often older and already in work, and the competition for the remaining entry-level places is intense. That is not a criticism of Multiverse alone: it is a feature of the English apprenticeship system as a whole, where the majority of starts are now by people aged 25 and over. But it matters for candidates who read the company's early story and expect it to describe the present.

## The scale, and the scrutiny

Multiverse's scale, as the company reports it, is considerable. The company claims more than 1,500 employer partners and more than 22,000 people placed into apprenticeships, figures reported in January 2026. Later reporting in 2026 put the number trained at around 30,000 apprentices. These two figures measure different things, and the difference matters: the first counts people placed into apprenticeships, the second counts people trained, including the upskilling of existing staff that is now the core of the business. A reader who treats the larger number as an update of the smaller one would be misled. Both are company figures, and neither has been independently audited in public.

The scrutiny arrived in 2026, and it is serious. Ofsted, the English education inspectorate, rated Multiverse Outstanding in 2021. Its 2026 report card graded four of five areas as Needs attention, a sharp downgrade. Ofsted also noted that just under half of the apprentices who started in 2024/25 left early. Department for Education figures put Multiverse's completion rate at 52.6%, against a sector average of 65.4%. That is a gap of nearly 13 percentage points, and it is the figure that should sit next to every claim about scale.

![Scale vs scrutiny: more than 1,500 employer partners and 22,000 people placed, company figures, against a 52.6% completion rate vs a 65.4% sector average, Department for Education figures, 2026](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/luca-151/candidate-assessment-guide-hashnode/main/images/article-07-scrutiny.svg align="center")

It is worth being careful about what these numbers do and do not show. A completion rate measures whether apprentices finish, not whether those who finish do well, and it can be depressed by factors outside a provider's control, including employers withdrawing learners. The sector average itself, at 65.4%, is not a high bar: roughly a third of apprentices across England do not complete. Multiverse is below that bar by a wide margin, and the Ofsted grades and the early leaving figures point in the same direction, which makes it harder to dismiss the completion rate as a statistical quirk. At the same time, the company's own figures, such as the reported 92% completion rate for StackFuel, its German acquisition, suggest that completion varies a great deal by programme and by country, and that the English apprenticeship figures are the problem area rather than the whole business.

None of this makes Multiverse a bad provider, and none of it makes the apprenticeship route a bad choice. It makes the provider a mixed one, with real scale and real quality problems, and it makes the choice of provider matter more than the choice of route. That is the even-handed reading the evidence supports: a company that has grown fast, raised more money than any British edtech before it, and is now being held to account by the regulators of the system it operates in.

## The partners and the state

The client list is a large part of the company's credibility. Microsoft, Databricks and Palantir are named as platform partners. Among employers running programmes are the AA, Capita, Addison Lee, Babcock, Age UK, John Lewis and M&S. That range, from breakdown cover to retail to defence, is a strength: it shows the model is not confined to technology companies, which matters for candidates who want an apprenticeship outside the tech sector.

In 2026 the UK Government announced a partnership with Multiverse as part of its move towards AI upskilling. For a company whose model depends on levy funding, a government partnership is both a commercial win and a signal: the state sees apprenticeship providers as a delivery mechanism for its AI skills agenda. It also raises the stakes of the scrutiny described above. A provider that carries a government partnership and a Needs attention grade from Ofsted is a provider whose quality will be watched, and candidates should watch it too.

## What it means for candidates

**A levy-funded programme costs you nothing, and that is a real advantage.** If you are offered a Multiverse apprenticeship, you pay no tuition. That is worth real money compared with a degree, and it is the strongest part of the proposition. But free is not the same as good, and the absence of a price means you have to judge quality by other measures.

**Check completion rates and Ofsted grades before choosing a provider.** The Department for Education publishes completion rates, and Ofsted publishes grades, for every provider. Multiverse's completion rate is 52.6% against a sector average of 65.4%, and its 2026 Ofsted grades were Needs attention in four of five areas. Ask any provider, including Multiverse, what its completion rate is for the specific programme you are considering, and what it does to support learners who are at risk of leaving early. A provider that cannot answer that question clearly is telling you something.

**Understand who the apprenticeship is for now.** Apprenticeships increasingly go to existing employees, and Multiverse's own pivot reflects that. If you are a school leaver, the market you are entering is not the one WhiteHat launched into in 2016. Entry-level places exist, and the employers named in this article show they exist at large organisations, but they are competitive and they are a shrinking share of the total. If you are already in work and your employer offers an AI or data upskilling programme, you are now the core customer, and the question to ask is whether the programme will change what you can do at work, not whether it carries a fashionable brand.

**Treat the degree question as a trade, not a verdict.** Multiverse now awards its own BSc degrees, which underlines the point: the choice is no longer between a degree and an apprenticeship, but between different routes to a degree-level credential, with different costs, different employers attached and different completion risks. Compare the routes on those terms: total cost to you, the employer you will work for, the credential at the end, and the chance you actually finish.

**Watch the pivot, not the pitch.** The company's funding, its acquisitions and its government partnership all point the same way: toward AI upskilling of people already in work. That is a legitimate business, and for many candidates it is the more useful one. But if you are choosing based on the original story, the school leaver alternative to university, check that the specific programme in front of you still matches that story before you commit.
