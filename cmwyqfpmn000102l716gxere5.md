# UK Graduate Hiring in 2026: 141 Applications per Vacancy

*Last checked: 27 November 2026 · 7 min read*

> **How we cover this.** This is a data-led hiring market piece. Every figure comes from a named survey or dataset, with the publisher and the date, and the text says where two sources disagree rather than picking the friendlier number.

## Hiring is up, and it has never been harder to get hired

UK graduate hiring grew 4% in 2025/26, after falling 8% the year before. That is the headline from the Institute of Student Employers (ISE) Student Recruitment Survey 2026, published on 14 October 2026 and covering 155 employers, more than 1.7 million applications and 31,577 hires and work experience placements. Employers in the survey forecast a further 7% rise in graduate hiring for 2026/27.

Read one line further and the headline starts to come apart. The growth came from a small number of large employers: without them, hiring grew 0.8%, and the typical employer's intake did not change at all. 45% of employers increased graduate hiring, 15% held it level, and 40% cut it. This is a market moving in two directions at once, and which direction you face depends on which employers you apply to.

And then there is the number that defines the year for candidates: **141 applications for every graduate vacancy**, the highest figure the ISE has recorded since it began tracking graduate recruitment more than 30 years ago. Two decades ago the figure was 38. In 2022/23 it was 86. It sat at 140 for two cycles before this year's 141.

## Where the applications land

The national average hides sharp differences by place and sector. London graduate vacancies drew 172 applications each. Financial and professional services drew 158. Digital and IT roles drew about 205, the highest of any sector in the survey. An apprenticeship vacancy drew 96, still high, but noticeably less crowded than a graduate role.

The volume is not only about graduates. Applications for internships rose 27% and applications for undergraduate placements rose 15%, while internship and placement hiring fell 8%. More students are chasing fewer early rungs on the ladder, and the squeeze starts before graduation.

Individual employers illustrate the scale. EDF opened its graduate scheme in September 2026 and closed the window early after 12,000 applications, a 10% rise on the year before, according to reporting on the scheme.

## The other number: adverts are down sharply

There is a second dataset that tells a different story, and it deserves the same attention. Adzuna, the job search engine, counted 7,826 graduate vacancies advertised in September 2026, which is 43.1% fewer than a year earlier.

Both numbers can be true, because they count different things. The ISE counts hires made by the large employers that answer its survey, and those employers are still hiring. Adzuna counts vacancies advertised on the open market, and that pool has shrunk sharply. The gap between them is itself informative: the graduate market is concentrating in a smaller number of large employers running structured schemes, while the broader advertised market contracts. Candidates who apply only through advertised vacancies see a much smaller market than candidates who also target employers' own schemes directly.

The US picture, for comparison, is brighter on paper: NACE, the US National Association of Colleges and Employers, reported that US employers planned 5.6% more graduate hires. But the competition dynamic is the same, and the UK figures show where the pressure concentrates.

## AI is now part of almost every application

The survey's other headline is about AI, and it cuts both ways. 92% of employers suspected or confirmed that candidates were using AI in assessment and selection, up from 85% the year before. The student side of the research shows the heaviest use comes from high-volume applicants, for whom an AI drafting aid becomes a spray tool: the same application, lightly rewritten, sent to hundreds of roles.

That volume is part of why applications per vacancy keep climbing. AI has lowered the cost of applying to near zero, so application counts rise even where hiring does not. Employers know this, and it is changing how they filter: the ISE's reporting notes that mass applications are reshaping early careers recruitment, with employers leaning harder on assessments and screening stages that cannot be completed by pasting a generated answer.

Employers themselves are slower to adopt AI on their side. Only 16% of employers in the survey currently use AI in recruitment. And in the ISE's Student Development Survey 2026, covering 144 employers, 40% expected AI to replace no early careers roles and a further 42% expected it to replace only a small number (1 to 10%). The fear that AI will erase entry-level hiring is not what employers themselves report. What AI is changing, in their view, is what early career hires do once hired, rather than whether they are hired at all.

For candidates, the practical point is about disclosure and judgement. Most large employers now ask whether AI was used, and several have disqualified candidates for undisclosed use. Using AI to draft is common; submitting AI output as your own thinking, in a process designed to test your thinking, is the behaviour employers are screening for.

## Pay: the median starting salary

The ISE's 2025 survey gave a median starting salary of £33,000 for graduates. That figure is a year old and covers large employers with structured schemes, so it sits above what most graduates actually earn in their first role. It is useful as a benchmark for the schemes in this survey, not as a picture of the whole graduate labour market.

## What it means for candidates

**Apply to schemes, not just to adverts.** The ISE employers are hiring, and their schemes are where the growth is. The advertised vacancy count is falling, so a strategy built only on job boards sees the worst of the market.

**Treat volume as a cost, not a strategy.** With 141 applications per vacancy, sending more applications is the weakest lever available. Employers are screening harder precisely because volume has exploded. A smaller number of tailored applications, each with a real answer to why this employer, will outperform a hundred near-identical ones, and it is also the behaviour that survives AI screening.

**Consider apprenticeships as a parallel route.** An apprenticeship vacancy drew 96 applications against 141 for a graduate role, and apprenticeship hiring held up while internship hiring fell. For school leavers and for graduates in unrelated subjects, the apprenticeship route is less crowded and leads to the same employers.

**Prepare for the assessment, not just the application.** With application volumes at record levels, the filter has moved downstream: the assessment stages are where the cut happens. The same employers reporting 141 applications per vacancy are the ones running game-based assessments, video interviews and assessment centres. That is where preparation pays.

**Do not read the 4% as a recovery.** Hiring rose after an 8% fall, the growth is concentrated in a few large employers, 40% of employers cut intake, and the advertised market shrank by more than 40% year on year. The honest description is stabilisation at a very high level of competition, not a recovery. The forecast 7% rise for 2026/27 is, again, driven by a few employers. Candidates should plan for a market that stays crowded.
